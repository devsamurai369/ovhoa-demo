Rule Books

Know which rulebook governs your game. OVHOA officials work under three different rule sets depending on the league — USA Hockey rules for youth games, NCAA rules for ACHA and USPHL games, and NFHS/OHSAA regulations for high school games.

Start Here

Rules Comparison: USA Hockey vs. NFHS vs. NCAA

If you work more than one level, this is the most useful document on this page. Published by the USA Hockey and NFHS Playing Rules Committees, it puts the three rule sets side by side — rink, equipment, penalties, officiating systems and playing rules — so you can see exactly what changes when you step onto a different sheet.

A sample only — the full chart runs 11 pages. Throughout it, a Match Penalty (USA Hockey) and a Disqualification (NFHS & NCAA) are treated as identical: a 5-minute penalty, immediate ejection, and additional suspension.

One caution on the NCAA column. This is the 2025–26 edition of the chart, built against the 2024–26 NCAA rules. The NCAA has since published the 2026–28 rulebook, which changed several items shown here — including hand passes (now permitted in the defensive zone), checking from behind (“in open ice” removed), the goalkeeper’s privileged area (removed), the definition of fighting, and offside (the puck must now be controlled with the stick). For NCAA, ACHA and USPHL games, treat the 2026–28 NCAA rulebook as authoritative.

USA Hockey

Governs: all USA Hockey sanctioned youth, high school club, and adult games.

The current rulebook is the 2025–29 USA Hockey Playing Rules. Every registered official needs it — it is also the reference for your annual Playing Rules Exercise.

USA Hockey Rulebook

NCAA Rules

Governs: ACHA and USPHL games. If you are working collegiate club or USPHL junior hockey, this is your rulebook — not USA Hockey’s.

Major rule changes in the 2026–28 NCAA rulebook
  • Helmets (Rule 9.4): A player who intentionally removes their helmet during play is assessed a minor penalty. A goalkeeper who intentionally removes their helmet/facemask on an opposing breakaway concedes an awarded goal.
  • Coincidental penalties (Rule 19): In the last five minutes of regulation or any time in overtime, a minor to Team A and a major to Team B at the same stoppage means the three-minute differential (one minute against a double minor) is served immediately.
  • Major penalties (Rule 20): A second major penalty in the same game is an automatic game misconduct.
  • Goalkeeper penalties (Rule 28.2): A goalkeeper is not sent to the penalty bench for an offense incurring a major and/or misconduct penalty.
  • Goalkeeper’s privileged area (Rule 28.4): Removed.
  • Fighting (Rule 48.1): Fighting is defined as punching or attempting to punch an opponent repeatedly; a single punch is penalized as roughing.
  • Checking from behind (Rule 50): “In open ice” removed from the rule.
  • Hitting after the whistle (Rule 53.3): Eliminated as its own infraction — classified as roughing or unsportsmanlike conduct.
  • Throwing the stick (Rule 55.5): Throwing a stick outside the playing area is upgraded from a misconduct to a game misconduct.
  • Spitting (Rule 79.5): Penalized as a game disqualification.
  • Faceoffs (Rule 81): The blade of the stick must be flat on the ice.
  • Offside (Rule 86.1): A player must control the puck with their stick before their skates enter the offensive zone.
  • Hand pass (Rule 84): Hand passes initiated and completed in the defensive zone are permitted.
  • Delay of game (new rule): A dump-in from the attacking team’s own side of center that the goalkeeper freezes results in an end-zone faceoff in the defending zone with no defensive substitutions.
  • Timeouts (Rule 92.2): Not usable when your team cannot change players due to an infraction; one timeout per stoppage; no extra regular-season overtime timeout.
  • Coach’s challenge (Rule 93.4): A disallowed-goal minor for goalkeeper interference may be challenged; one challenge per stoppage per team.
NCAA penalty terminology for USA Hockey officials

Game misconduct (Rule 22): Suspension for the balance of the game; a substitute may replace the player immediately. With a major + game misconduct, the team places a substitute in the box to serve the major.

Disqualification (Rule 23): The NCAA’s equivalent of a match-type removal — the player is removed for the remainder of the game plus a major penalty, with progressive suspensions for repeat disqualifications (first DQ: that game plus one; second: plus two; and so on). DQs carry over season to season for players with remaining eligibility.

There is no “match penalty” category in the NCAA book — conduct that draws a match penalty under USA Hockey rules is generally a disqualification under NCAA rules.

ACHA

Governs: American Collegiate Hockey Association (collegiate club) games.

The ACHA plays under the 2026–28 NCAA rulebook with its own published guidance. All six ACHA divisions — Men’s D1, D2 and D3, Women’s D1 and D2, and the ACDC — have approved the rules below, so they apply to every ACHA game you work.

Fighting is handled differently here than in the USPHL. In ACHA games the penalty is a major plus game disqualification. The USPHL, working from the same NCAA rulebook, substitutes a game misconduct instead. If you work both leagues, this is the difference most likely to catch you out.

ACHA guidance on the 2026–28 NCAA rules
  • Starting lineup (7.2): if the team scoring on the first shift challenges the opponent’s lineup and it was wrong, the goal nullifies the bench minor and no further penalties are assessed.
  • Helmets (9.4): intentionally removing a helmet during play is a minor. A goalkeeper who removes their helmet or facemask on an opposing breakaway concedes an awarded goal. See also 67.1.
  • Coincidental penalties (19): in the last five minutes of regulation or any time in overtime, a minor (or double minor) to one team and a major (or match) to the other at the same stoppage means the three-minute (or one-minute) differential is served immediately as a major. The team receiving the major must place the replacement in the box before the penalty expires — immediately in the case of a major plus game misconduct or disqualification. Applies regardless of on-ice strength; examples are in Appendix G, page 138.
  • Major penalties (20): a second major in the same game is an automatic game misconduct.
  • Goalkeeper penalties (28.2): a goalkeeper is not sent to the box for a major or misconduct — it is served immediately by a teammate who was on the ice, designated by the coach through the captain, and that substitute may not be changed. If the goalkeeper’s major plus game misconduct is coincidental with a major or game misconduct to the other team, nobody serves it, since the goalkeeper has been ejected.
  • Fighting (48.1): defined as one player punching or attempting to punch an opponent repeatedly, or two players wrestling such that linespersons cannot separate them. A single punch is roughing. Penalty — major plus game disqualification.
  • Sucker punch (53.3) — women’s game only: a major to a player who punches an unsuspecting or unwilling opponent in a way that could cause injury.
  • Head-butting (49): a minor may be called for an attempted or non-forcible head-butt; reserve major and game misconduct for a forcible or egregious one.
  • Checking from behind (50): “in open ice” removed from the rule.
  • Hitting after the whistle (53.3): eliminated as its own infraction — call it roughing or unsportsmanlike conduct.
  • Throwing the stick (55.5): outside the playing area is now a game misconduct rather than a misconduct.
  • Goalkeeper leaving stick (55): if a goalkeeper leaving the ice leaves a stick or equipment, or piles snow, and it stops an imminent goal, a goal is awarded.
  • Interference (59.1): any defending-team member, including a coach or non-playing person, who throws or shoots a stick or object at the puck or puck carrier in their defending zone concedes a penalty shot.
  • Obstruction/Interference (60): removed from the 2026–28 rulebook.
  • Awarded goal (67.5): on a breakaway, a player or goalkeeper may not deliberately displace the goal post or remove their helmet/facemask.
  • No line change (67.6): on a dump-in from the attacking team’s own side of the centre red line that the goalkeeper freezes, the face-off is in the goalkeeper’s defending zone with no defensive substitutions — and the attacking team chooses which end-zone dot.
  • Offensive language (79.3): renamed from “ethnic/racial slurs”. Covers profane, threatening, vulgar or derogatory remarks relating to race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, gender, gender expression, gender identity, sexual orientation or disability. Penalty — game disqualification, except medical personnel, who are subject to supplementary discipline.
  • Spitting (79.5): on an official, player or non-playing person — major and game disqualification.
  • Face-offs (81): the full blade must be flat on the ice inside the designated white area. At the eight face-off spots the defending player places their stick first; at centre ice the visiting player goes first.
  • Hand pass (84): play is not stopped for a hand pass by players in their own defending zone. The puck’s location when contacted by either player determines the zone.
  • High stick (84.4): on a high-sticking-the-puck violation by the defending team in its own zone, the offending team may change players.
  • Face-off location after a hand pass or high stick (84.3/84.4): the nearest dot to the infraction with the least territorial advantage gained.
  • Offside (86.1): a player must control the puck with their stick before their skates enter the offensive zone. A player controlling the puck who crosses the line ahead of it is not offside provided they had possession and control with the stick before both skates fully crossed the leading edge of the blue line.
  • Time-outs (92.1): one 60-second time-out per team in regular-season games. Only one time-out — team or commercial — per stoppage, and a commercial time-out counts as an official time-out charged to neither team. No back-to-back time-outs, none after a face-off violation, none once penalty-shot instructions have been given, and none during a shootout. New: none granted to the defending team when it may not substitute under Rule 80.3.
  • Overtime time-outs (92.2): new — no additional time-outs in regular-season overtime when the game is tied after 60 minutes.
  • Shootout eligibility (91.2): every player may shoot unless serving a 10-minute misconduct or assessed a game misconduct or disqualification. When a goalkeeper takes a misconduct, the designated server becomes ineligible; if that happens during the shootout, the designated server must be a player who has not yet shot, and they become ineligible.

Overtime and shootout formats vary by division — check the ACHA manual or the overtime/shootout guidelines on the ACHA officials page.

Women’s divisions: body checking and the 2026–27 point of emphasis

Rule 94.1 — body checking is not permitted anywhere on the ice. It occurs when a player delivers a distinct and definable moment of impact using overt hip, shoulder, arm or torso action to separate the puck carrier from the puck. Penalty — a minor, a major and game misconduct, or a major and disqualification, at the referee’s discretion.

The NCAA’s 2026–27 point of emphasis modernises how Rule 94 is applied, rewarding superior angling and positioning rather than penalising all contact. Legal body contact requires the initiating player to keep a skating lane consistent with the puck’s location, deliver shoulder-to-shoulder contact from even with or ahead of the opponent, avoid contact from behind where the opponent cannot expect it, keep the stick on the ice or ready to play the puck, and avoid both an overt extension to deliver force and any explosive upward or outward motion.

Four scenarios are classified as legal contact:

  • Parallel possession pursuit — skating the same direction, a defender even with or ahead of the carrier leans them off the puck shoulder-to-shoulder. Legal with no extension or opposite-direction motion. Contact from behind the carrier is a body check, as the carrier is defenceless.
  • The end-wall pursuit — guiding an opponent into the boards with a hand on the hip, stick on the ice, releasing once momentum is neutralised. This is “steering”, not boarding or checking, provided there is no excessive shove.
  • The forward angle and the cut-back — a defender who has established a legal angling route is entitled to their skating lane and need not vacate it if the carrier cuts back into it.
  • The 50/50 puck battle — contact that is a byproduct of winning the lane, without an opposite-direction hit with extension, is not a penalty.

Guidance to officials: judge the action, not the outcome. The deciding factor between a minor for body checking and legal body contact is the delivery — a player may position their body to impede, but may not propel their body to impact. Contact without puck possession falls under Rule 59, interference.

Penalty reporting for ACHA games is on our File a Report page. Questions on ACHA rule application go to our Referee-in-Chief, Ken Handley.

USPHL

Governs: USPHL (NCDC and Premier) junior games.

The USPHL plays under the 2026–28 NCAA rulebook with league-specific exceptions. The official 2026–27 Officiating Team Handbook below covers the league’s protocols and variances — read it in full before your first USPHL assignment.

Key USPHL variances from NCAA rules
  • Fighting: In lieu of a game disqualification, a player receives a game misconduct. Any secondary fight is a major + disqualification. Non-participating players must clear to their benches (Rule 48.2) — strictly enforced.
  • Helmet removal in an altercation: Treated as grasping the facemask (Rule 47.1) and penalized as a disqualification — no game-misconduct option.
  • Coincidental minors: On-ice strength stays 5-on-5 in all divisions.
  • High-sticking a player with a half shield: Escalating options based on severity — minor (no injury), double minor (accidental), major + game misconduct, or major + disqualification.
  • Overtime: Games cannot end in a tie — 5-minute 3-on-3 overtime, then a 3-man shootout, then sudden-death shootout with new shooters until the roster is exhausted.
  • Mercy rule (Premier): After the first period, an 8+ goal differential runs the clock (except power plays). Any major during mercy-rule time is an automatic disqualification.
  • Video review (NCDC only): One coach’s challenge per game, contested goals only; officials may not initiate a review.
